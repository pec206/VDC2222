Extraction Aftercare & Recovery

Your complete guide to recovering after a tooth extraction — protecting the blood clot, managing pain and swelling, avoiding dry socket, and understanding the healing timeline.

Immediately After Extraction

The first hour after your extraction is critical. A blood clot must form in the empty socket to protect the bone and nerve endings underneath. This clot is the foundation of the entire healing process.

  • Bite firmly on the gauze pad placed by your surgeon for 30 to 45 minutes. Apply steady, even pressure — do not chew on it
  • If bleeding continues after removing the first gauze, place a fresh piece of damp gauze over the site and bite firmly for another 30 minutes
  • Do not spit, rinse, or use a straw for the rest of the day — the suction can dislodge the blood clot
  • Do not touch the extraction site with your tongue or fingers
  • Some oozing is normal — your saliva may appear pink or blood-tinged for 12 to 24 hours. This is not the same as active bleeding

The First 24 Hours

The first day after extraction is about protecting the blood clot and keeping inflammation under control. Follow these rules strictly:

  • No rinsing or spitting — even gentle rinsing can dislodge the blood clot
  • No straws — the suction force pulls the clot out of the socket
  • No smoking — smoking is the single biggest risk factor for dry socket. The heat and chemicals impair blood flow and healing. Avoid smoking for at least 72 hours, ideally longer
  • Rest with your head elevated — prop your head up with extra pillows when lying down to reduce blood flow to the area and minimise swelling
  • No strenuous exercise — elevated heart rate and blood pressure can restart bleeding. Light walking is fine
  • Avoid hot foods and drinks — heat increases blood flow and can dissolve the clot. Stick to cool and lukewarm foods
  • No alcohol for at least 24 hours, as it thins the blood and interferes with clot formation

Managing Pain & Swelling

Some pain and swelling after a tooth extraction is completely normal. Here is how to manage both effectively:

Pain Relief

  • Ibuprofen (400mg) — take every 6 to 8 hours with food. Start taking it before the anaesthesia wears off for the best effect. Ibuprofen reduces both pain and inflammation
  • Paracetamol (500–1000mg) — can be taken alongside ibuprofen if needed. Do not exceed 4g per day
  • If your surgeon prescribed a stronger pain reliever, take it as directed and only if over-the-counter options are insufficient

Swelling Management

  • Ice pack — apply to the outside of your cheek, 20 minutes on, 20 minutes off, for the first 24 to 48 hours
  • Swelling typically peaks on day 2 to 3 and then gradually subsides over the following 3 to 5 days
  • After 48 hours, switch from ice to a warm compress if swelling persists — warmth helps increase blood flow and aids healing at this stage
  • Some bruising on the cheek or jaw is normal, especially after wisdom tooth or surgical extractions, and will resolve on its own

Eating & Drinking

Proper nutrition supports healing, but you need to be careful about what you eat and how you eat it:

Day 1: Cold Soft Foods

  • Cold yoghurt, ice cream (no chunks), chilled smoothies (no straw — use a spoon)
  • Cold soup, applesauce, pudding
  • Avoid anything hot, spicy, acidic, or crunchy
  • Chew only on the opposite side of your mouth

Days 2–3: Warm Soft Foods

  • Warm (not hot) soup, mashed potatoes, scrambled eggs
  • Soft pasta, porridge, steamed vegetables
  • Continue avoiding hard, crunchy, or chewy foods

Days 4–7: Gradually Resume Normal Diet

  • Introduce firmer foods as comfort allows
  • Continue to avoid chewing directly on the extraction site
  • Avoid small, hard foods (seeds, nuts, popcorn) that could lodge in the socket

Dry Socket — What It Is & How to Avoid It

Dry socket (alveolar osteitis) occurs when the blood clot is lost or dissolves before the wound has healed, exposing the underlying bone and nerves. It is the most common complication after extraction and causes intense, throbbing pain that typically begins 2 to 4 days after the procedure.

How to Prevent Dry Socket

Follow these rules carefully: do not smoke for at least 72 hours (the most important single factor); do not use straws, spit, or rinse forcefully for 24 hours; avoid dislodging the clot with your tongue or fingers; take all medications as directed; and eat soft foods for the first few days. If you develop severe, radiating pain 2 to 4 days after extraction that painkillers do not relieve, contact the clinic immediately — dry socket is treatable but requires professional care.

Oral Hygiene During Recovery

Keeping your mouth clean is essential for preventing infection, but you must be gentle around the extraction site:

  • Day 1 — do not brush the teeth adjacent to the extraction site. Brush your other teeth gently as normal
  • After 24 hours — begin gentle saltwater rinses (half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water). Do not swish vigorously; let the water flow gently over the area and then let it fall out of your mouth
  • After 48 hours — resume careful brushing of all teeth, including those near the extraction site. Use a soft-bristle toothbrush and avoid pressing into the socket
  • Continue saltwater rinses 3 to 4 times daily, especially after meals, for 5 to 7 days
  • Avoid alcohol-based mouthwash for the first week, as it can irritate the healing tissue

Wisdom Tooth Specific Recovery

Wisdom tooth extractions, particularly surgical (impacted) extractions, involve more extensive tissue and bone work. Recovery typically takes longer than a simple extraction:

  • Swelling is more pronounced — expect significant swelling on days 2 to 3, especially for lower wisdom teeth. This is normal and will subside over 5 to 7 days
  • Limited jaw opening (trismus) is common and can last 7 to 10 days. Gently stretch your jaw by slowly opening and closing your mouth several times a day
  • Stitches — if dissolvable sutures were placed, they typically dissolve within 7 to 14 days. Do not pull on them. If non-dissolvable sutures were used, a follow-up appointment will be scheduled to remove them
  • Numbness or tingling in the lower lip, chin, or tongue can occasionally occur after lower wisdom tooth removal if the nerve is near the tooth root. This is usually temporary and resolves within days to weeks
  • Difficulty swallowing or a sore throat can occur after lower wisdom tooth extraction due to swelling near the throat muscles. This resolves within a few days

Warning Signs

Contact Vision Dental Clinic immediately if you experience any of the following:

  • Excessive bleeding that does not stop after 2 hours of firm gauze pressure
  • Increasing pain after day 3 — pain should be improving, not worsening. Worsening pain may indicate dry socket or infection
  • Fever (above 38°C / 100.4°F) — a sign of possible infection
  • Swelling that worsens after day 3 or begins to spread
  • Numbness lasting more than 24 hours after the anaesthesia should have worn off
  • Pus or foul taste coming from the extraction site
  • Difficulty breathing or swallowing — seek emergency care immediately

Healing Timeline

Understanding the healing stages helps you know what to expect and when to be concerned:

TimeframeWhat HappensWhat You Should Do
Day 1Blood clot forms in the socket. Bleeding slows and stops.Bite gauze, rest, ice pack, soft cold foods only.
Days 2–3Swelling peaks. Bruising may appear. Discomfort is at its highest.Continue ice/warm compress, pain relief, saltwater rinses begin.
Days 4–7Swelling begins to subside. Pain decreases noticeably. Socket starts to close.Gradually resume normal foods. Continue gentle hygiene.
Weeks 1–2Soft tissue (gum) closes over the socket. Sutures dissolve or are removed.Resume normal brushing. Monitor for any unusual symptoms.
Weeks 3–4Gum tissue largely healed. Socket filling in with new tissue.Normal activities and diet. Follow up with your dentist if advised.
Months 2–4Bone remodelling continues beneath the healed gum. The ridge may shrink slightly.Discuss tooth replacement options (implant, bridge, or denture).

Replacing the Missing Tooth

Once the extraction site has healed, it is important to consider replacing the missing tooth (unless it was a wisdom tooth). Leaving a gap can lead to shifting of adjacent teeth, bone loss in the jaw, and bite problems over time. Your options include:

  • Dental Implant — a titanium post placed in the jawbone that acts as an artificial root, topped with a crown. The most durable and natural-looking option
  • Dental Bridge — a fixed restoration that uses adjacent teeth as supports. A good option when neighbouring teeth also need crowns
  • Denture — a removable option, suitable when multiple teeth are missing or as a temporary solution while waiting for implant placement

Common Questions

Extraction Aftercare FAQs

How long does it take to recover from a tooth extraction?
For a simple extraction, most patients feel significantly better within 3 to 5 days. The gum tissue typically closes over the socket within 1 to 2 weeks. Surgical or wisdom tooth extractions may take 7 to 10 days for initial recovery. Complete bone healing beneath the gum takes 2 to 4 months.
What is dry socket and how do I prevent it?
Dry socket occurs when the blood clot in the extraction site is lost or dissolves prematurely, exposing bone and nerves. It causes severe, throbbing pain starting 2 to 4 days after extraction. Prevent it by not smoking for at least 72 hours, avoiding straws and spitting for 24 hours, and following all aftercare instructions carefully.
When can I eat normally after an extraction?
Start with cold soft foods on day 1, move to warm soft foods on days 2 to 3, and gradually reintroduce firmer foods from day 4 onward as comfort allows. Most patients return to a normal diet within 7 to 10 days. Avoid small hard foods like seeds and nuts near the socket until it is fully closed.
Is bleeding after a tooth extraction normal?
Light oozing and blood-tinged saliva are normal for 12 to 24 hours. Bite on gauze for 30 to 45 minutes with firm, steady pressure. If active bleeding continues after 2 hours of gauze pressure, contact the clinic. Avoid rinsing, spitting, using straws, and strenuous activity for the first 24 hours, as these can restart bleeding.
Do I need to replace the extracted tooth?
Wisdom teeth generally do not need replacement. For any other tooth, replacement is strongly recommended to prevent adjacent teeth from shifting, bone loss in the jaw, and bite problems. Options include dental implants (the most durable solution), bridges, or dentures. Your surgeon will discuss the best option during your follow-up.
