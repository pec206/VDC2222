Scaling and Root Planing
vs Regular Dental Cleaning

Two Types of Cleaning -- One Important Difference

If your dentist has ever told you that you need a "deep cleaning" instead of a regular one, you may have wondered what the difference is. Both procedures aim to keep your teeth and gums healthy, but they serve different purposes and target different problems. Understanding the distinction can help you take better care of your oral health and know when to seek the right treatment.

At Vision Dental Clinic in Abu Dhabi, our periodontal team sees patients every day who are unsure whether they need a routine cleaning or something more involved. This guide breaks down both procedures so you can make informed decisions about your dental care.

What Is a Regular Cleaning (Prophylaxis)?

A regular dental cleaning, known professionally as prophylaxis, is a preventive procedure performed during your routine dental visit. It is designed for patients who have generally healthy gums or only mild plaque buildup.

During a regular cleaning, your dentist or hygienist will:

  • Remove plaque and tartar from the surfaces of your teeth, particularly along and just above the gum line
  • Polish your teeth to remove surface stains and create a smooth surface that resists future plaque buildup
  • Floss between all teeth to remove any remaining debris
  • Check for early signs of gum disease, cavities, and other oral health concerns

A prophylaxis appointment usually takes 30 to 45 minutes and is typically recommended every six months for most adults. It is a straightforward, comfortable procedure that does not require anaesthesia. Think of it as routine maintenance -- keeping everything in good working order before problems develop.

What Is Scaling and Root Planing (Deep Cleaning)?

Scaling and root planing is a more intensive, therapeutic procedure used to treat gum disease that has already developed. Unlike a regular cleaning, it goes below the gum line to address bacteria and deposits that have accumulated in the periodontal pockets -- the spaces between your gums and teeth that deepen when disease is present.

The procedure has two parts:

Scaling

Scaling involves removing plaque, tartar (calculus), and bacterial toxins from the tooth surfaces both above and below the gum line. Your dentist uses specialised instruments -- including ultrasonic scalers and hand instruments -- to reach into the periodontal pockets and clean areas that a regular cleaning cannot access. For a detailed look at what this involves, visit our scaling service page.

Root Planing

After scaling, root planing smooths the rough surfaces of the tooth roots. Bacteria tend to collect in these rough areas, so smoothing them helps the gums reattach to the teeth and reduces the depth of the periodontal pockets. This step is critical for long-term healing.

Deep cleaning is usually performed under local anaesthesia for your comfort. Depending on the extent of the disease, it may be completed in one visit or divided into two to four appointments, treating one section of the mouth at a time.

Deep cleaning is not a more thorough version of a regular cleaning. It is a specific treatment for gum disease that addresses infection below the gum line.

Key Differences Between Regular Cleaning and Deep Cleaning

While both procedures involve removing plaque and tartar, the similarities end there. Here are the main differences:

  • Purpose: Regular cleaning is preventive. Deep cleaning is therapeutic -- it treats existing gum disease.
  • Where it cleans: Regular cleaning targets above and at the gum line. Deep cleaning goes below the gum line into periodontal pockets.
  • Who needs it: Regular cleaning is for patients with healthy gums or gingivitis. Deep cleaning is for patients diagnosed with periodontitis (pocket depths of 4 mm or more).
  • Anaesthesia: Regular cleaning rarely requires numbing. Deep cleaning typically uses local anaesthesia.
  • Duration: Regular cleaning takes 30 to 45 minutes in a single visit. Deep cleaning may require multiple visits of 60 to 90 minutes each.
  • Follow-up: After regular cleaning, you return in six months. After deep cleaning, you may need follow-up visits every three to four months for periodontal maintenance.

When Is Deep Cleaning Necessary?

Your dentist will recommend scaling and root planing when there are clear signs that gum disease has progressed beyond what a regular cleaning can address. Indicators include:

  • Pocket depths of 4 mm or more -- measured with a periodontal probe during your examination
  • Bleeding gums during probing or brushing
  • Visible tartar buildup below the gum line
  • Bone loss detected on dental imaging
  • Gum recession exposing the tooth roots
  • Persistent bad breath that does not improve with brushing and flossing

If you have been diagnosed with periodontitis -- even in its early stages -- deep cleaning is typically the first line of treatment. It is a non-surgical approach that can halt the progression of the disease and give your gums the chance to heal.

What to Expect During and After Deep Cleaning

During the Procedure

Your dentist will apply local anaesthesia to ensure you are comfortable throughout the procedure. You may feel some pressure as the instruments work below the gum line, but pain should not be a concern. The ultrasonic scaler uses gentle vibrations and water to break up tartar, while hand instruments provide precise cleaning of the root surfaces.

After the Procedure

It is normal to experience some mild effects in the days following deep cleaning:

  • Tenderness and sensitivity: Your gums may feel sore for a few days. Teeth may be temporarily sensitive to hot and cold. This usually resolves within one to two weeks.
  • Minor bleeding: Some bleeding when brushing is normal in the first few days. It should decrease steadily.
  • Swelling: Mild gum swelling is common and subsides quickly.

Your dentist may prescribe a mouth rinse or recommend a desensitising toothpaste. Avoid hard, crunchy, or very hot foods for the first 48 hours. Gentle brushing and careful flossing should continue as normal.

Most patients notice a significant improvement in gum health within four to six weeks after deep cleaning. The gums become firmer, less swollen, and pockets begin to shrink.

How Often Do You Need Each Type of Cleaning?

Regular Cleaning

For patients with healthy gums, a professional cleaning every six months is the standard recommendation. Some patients with a history of gum problems or higher risk factors may benefit from cleanings every four months, even after successful treatment.

Deep Cleaning and Periodontal Maintenance

After scaling and root planing, your dentist will schedule follow-up appointments -- typically every three to four months -- for periodontal maintenance. These visits are more thorough than a regular cleaning and include re-measuring pocket depths, monitoring gum attachment, and cleaning areas that remain at risk.

Over time, if your gum health stabilises and pocket depths return to normal (3 mm or less), your dentist may transition you back to regular six-month cleanings. However, patients with a history of periodontitis often benefit from maintaining the more frequent schedule long-term to prevent recurrence.

Prevention Is Always the Best Approach

The best way to avoid needing deep cleaning is to prevent gum disease from developing in the first place. This means:

  • Brushing twice daily with a soft-bristled brush, paying attention to the gum line
  • Flossing every day to clean between teeth where your brush cannot reach
  • Attending regular dental visits so your dentist can catch early signs of gum disease before it progresses
  • Avoiding tobacco, which significantly increases your risk of periodontal disease
  • Eating a balanced diet rich in vitamins C and D to support gum health
  • Managing health conditions like diabetes that affect your gums

If you are unsure whether you need a regular cleaning or a deep cleaning, the best step is to schedule an appointment. Our periodontal team will assess your gum health, measure pocket depths, and recommend the right treatment for your specific situation.

Regular cleanings keep healthy gums healthy. Deep cleanings treat gum disease that has already started. Both are essential tools in protecting your smile for the long term.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Is deep cleaning the same as regular cleaning?
No. A regular cleaning (prophylaxis) removes plaque and tartar from above the gum line. Deep cleaning (scaling and root planing) goes below the gum line to remove bacteria and deposits from the root surfaces and periodontal pockets.
Does deep cleaning hurt?
With local anaesthesia, deep cleaning is comfortable. You may feel some pressure, but the area is numbed so you should not experience pain during the procedure. Mild soreness afterwards is normal and resolves within a few days.
How often do I need deep cleaning?
It depends on your gum condition. Patients with periodontal disease typically need deep cleaning every 3 to 4 months initially, then the interval may be extended as gum health improves.
Can I go back to regular cleanings after deep cleaning?
Yes, if your gum health improves and pocket depths return to normal, your dentist may transition you back to regular cleanings every 6 months. Ongoing monitoring ensures the disease does not return.
What happens if I skip deep cleaning?
Without treatment, periodontal disease progresses. Bacteria continue to destroy the bone and tissue supporting your teeth, leading to deeper pockets, gum recession, loose teeth, and eventually tooth loss.
